Hierarchical Routing

A way to organize a large network into regions or areas so that routers do not need to keep equally detailed routing information about every router in the entire network.

Routing scalabilityRegions / areasRouting tablesExam-ready notes

1. Why hierarchical routing?

In a small network, a router may be able to maintain routes to every destination. As a network grows, the number of routers and destinations grows too. Large routing tables consume memory, and exchanging detailed routing information can create overhead.

Hierarchical routing addresses this scaling problem by dividing the network into regions, areas, or levels. A router maintains detailed information about destinations in its own region and summarized or less detailed information about destinations outside it.

Key idea: Use detailed routes locally and more general routes for remote parts of the network. This reduces routing-table size, but may sometimes choose a route that is not globally shortest.

2. Conceptual network hierarchy

The diagram is a conceptual illustration of regions and a central interconnection layer. Actual network designs may use different names and structures.

  • Local / intra-region routing: routers use detailed information to reach destinations within their own region.
  • Inter-region routing: routers use routes or summaries to reach other regions through connecting routers.
  • Higher-level structure: regions connect through a backbone or higher-level routing layer.

Do not assume that every hierarchical network has exactly three levels. The number and names of levels depend on the network design.

3. How hierarchical routing works

  1. Divide the network: organize routers and destinations into regions or areas.
  2. Maintain local detail: routers keep detailed routing information for their own region.
  3. Represent remote destinations compactly: routes to outside regions can be summarized by region, prefix, or an exit/next-hop router, depending on the design.
  4. Forward within the region: if the destination is local, use the local routing information.
  5. Forward outside the region: send the packet toward an appropriate inter-region router or backbone route.
  6. Continue at the destination region: the remote region's routers use their local information to deliver the packet to the final destination.
Local destination → detailed local route
Remote destination → summarized / higher-level route

4. Simple forwarding example

Assume a network has three regions: A, B, and C. Host A7 is in Region A and Host C4 is in Region C.

  1. The source router checks whether C4 is in its own region. It is not.
  2. The router uses its inter-region routing information to send the packet toward the gateway/backbone route for Region C.
  3. Routers in the core forward the packet toward Region C using higher-level routes.
  4. Once the packet enters Region C, local routing information delivers it to C4.

This is a simplified conceptual example. It does not specify a particular routing protocol, addressing plan, or exact routing-table entries.

5. Routing-table size: the trade-off

Suppose a network contains many routers distributed across several regions. Without hierarchy, a router might need an entry for each destination or destination network. With hierarchy, it may keep detailed entries for local destinations and a smaller set of entries for remote regions.

6. Advantages

  • Scalability: supports the organization of large networks.
  • Smaller routing tables: remote destinations can be represented more compactly.
  • Less routing information to maintain: routers need not store every remote detail.
  • Administrative structure: regions can align with network boundaries or operational responsibilities.
  • Containment of local changes: with suitable design and summarization, some internal changes need not be represented in full detail outside the region.

7. Limitations

  • Potentially non-optimal paths: summarization can conceal a better route.
  • More planning: region boundaries, gateways, and summaries must be designed carefully.
  • Inter-region dependencies: connectivity between regions and the backbone becomes important.
  • Possible troubleshooting complexity: a route may depend on both local and higher-level decisions.

9. Exam-ready answers

3-mark answer: What is hierarchical routing?

Hierarchical routing is a routing approach in which a large network is divided into regions or levels. Routers maintain detailed routes for destinations in their own region and use summarized or higher-level routes for destinations in other regions. It helps reduce routing-table size and improves scalability.

5-mark answer: Explain hierarchical routing.

As a network grows, maintaining detailed routes to every destination increases routing-table size and routing-information overhead. Hierarchical routing divides the network into regions or areas. Routers use detailed information for local destinations and summarized or higher-level routes for remote destinations. Packets for local destinations are forwarded using local routes, while packets for remote destinations are sent toward an appropriate inter-region router or backbone. This improves scalability and reduces routing information, but route summarization may produce a path that is not globally shortest.

8–10-mark answer: Explain hierarchical routing with a diagram.

Begin with the definition and the scalability problem. Draw a network divided into regions connected through a backbone or higher-level layer. Explain local routing, inter-region routing, route summarization, and packet forwarding from one region to another. Discuss reduced routing-table size and lower information overhead as advantages. Explain that summarization may hide route details and result in non-optimal paths. End with a comparison to flat routing.
