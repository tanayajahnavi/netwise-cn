Load Shedding

Discarding selected packets when a network device or path is overloaded, so that remaining traffic can be handled with the resources available.

Congestion managementPacket droppingOverload responseExam-ready notes

1. What is load shedding?

Load shedding is a congestion-management technique in which a router or other network component deliberately discards some packets when it cannot handle all offered traffic. It is used to prevent overload from consuming all available buffer or processing resources.

Core idea: When all incoming packets cannot be served, discard selected packets according to a policy rather than allowing the device to become completely overwhelmed.

Load shedding is generally a response to severe load or resource shortage. It does not increase link capacity; it decides which traffic will not be carried when demand exceeds what can be served.

2. Visual working

  1. Traffic arrives at a router faster than it can be processed or transmitted.
  2. The queue grows and buffer space becomes scarce.
  3. The device applies a packet-discard policy.
  4. Selected packets are dropped, reducing the amount of traffic that must be stored or processed.
  5. The remaining packets continue through the network, subject to the available capacity.

Dropping packets may cause retransmissions or application-level loss. Load shedding is therefore an overload-management trade-off, not a way to make lost data disappear without consequences.

3. Why is load shedding used?

  • Protect finite buffers: router memory for queued packets is limited.
  • Prevent resource exhaustion: an overloaded device may not be able to handle every packet.
  • Preserve useful service: a suitable policy may prioritize packets or flows that are more important.
  • Reduce overload pressure: fewer packets need to be queued or processed.

4. Common packet-selection policies

When shedding is required, a system needs a rule for deciding which packets to discard. The precise policies depend on the network and traffic type.

Policy / approachBasic ideaPotential use or trade-off
Tail dropDrop newly arriving packets when the queue is full.Simple to implement, but may discard bursts of packets and does not explicitly distinguish traffic importance.
Priority-based droppingDiscard lower-priority packets before higher-priority packets.Can protect important traffic, but low-priority traffic may suffer more loss.
Class- or flow-aware sheddingApply a policy based on traffic class, flow, or application significance.Can align packet loss with service goals, but requires classification and policy configuration.
Random / probabilistic droppingDrop packets according to a probability or queue-management algorithm.Can avoid treating only the latest packet as the candidate for every drop; exact behaviour depends on the algorithm.

Tail drop is a common queue-overflow behaviour; the other rows describe general policy families. Do not assume all are listed as formal load-shedding categories in your lecturer's PPT unless shown there.

5. Simple example

A router can transmit 100 packets per time interval, but 140 packets arrive during that interval. If the queue cannot hold the excess and no additional capacity is available, the router cannot deliver every packet in that interval.

QuantityPackets per interval
Packets arriving140
Packets that can be served in the interval100
Excess demand relative to immediate service capacity40

The 40-packet difference is not automatically equal to the number dropped: a buffer may hold some packets for later service. If there is no remaining buffer space and the offered load persists, packets must be dropped or otherwise prevented from entering.

Important: Do not calculate packet loss as simply “arrivals − service capacity” unless the question explicitly says there is no queue space and asks about that interval. Queuing changes how many packets are actually dropped.

6. Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages

  • Helps a device cope with overload when resources are finite.
  • Can protect higher-priority or more useful traffic if the policy supports differentiation.
  • Prevents unbounded queue growth where buffering is limited.

Disadvantages

  • Causes packet loss and may require retransmission.
  • Can harm applications that require reliable delivery or continuous media if packets are discarded carelessly.
  • A poor policy may drop important traffic or unfairly penalize certain flows.
  • It treats the symptom of overload; capacity planning and other congestion-control mechanisms may still be needed.

7. Load shedding vs. traffic throttling

AspectLoad sheddingTraffic throttling
Main actionDiscard selected packets when the system cannot handle all traffic.Limit or regulate the rate at which traffic is offered or forwarded.
Packet handlingSelected packets are dropped.Depending on the mechanism, excess packets may be paced, delayed, queued, marked, or dropped.
EmphasisManage overload by deciding what not to carry.Reduce or control the rate of offered traffic.

8. Exam-ready answers

3-mark answer: Define load shedding.

Load shedding is a congestion-management technique in which a network device deliberately discards selected packets when offered traffic exceeds its available processing, transmission, or buffering resources. It helps the device cope with overload but causes packet loss.

5-mark answer: Explain load shedding.

When packets arrive faster than a router can process or transmit them, its queue grows and buffer space may become exhausted. The router applies a packet-discard policy to shed some traffic. The policy may be based on arrival order, priority, traffic class, or another configured rule. Dropping packets reduces the amount of traffic that must be stored or processed, but can cause loss and retransmissions.

8–10-mark answer: Explain load shedding with a diagram, policies, and limitations.

Define load shedding and explain the overload condition. Draw a diagram showing incoming traffic, a nearly full router buffer, the output link, and packets being discarded. Describe the steps from queue growth to packet selection and dropping. Explain relevant packet-selection policies such as tail drop and priority-based dropping if included in the course material. Conclude with benefits, packet-loss consequences, and the need for complementary congestion-management techniques.

Random Early Detection (RED)

  1. Maintain a running average of queue length.
  2. When the average rises above a threshold, mark congestion and randomly drop a small fraction of packets before the buffer is completely full.
  3. Random selection makes high-rate senders more likely to encounter a drop because they contribute more packets.
  4. A sender may infer loss from a missing acknowledgement and reduce its sending rate.

RED differs from tail drop, which waits until the buffer is full and then drops newly arriving packets.
