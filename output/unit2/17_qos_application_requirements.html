1. What is Quality of Service (QoS)?

Quality of Service (QoS) refers to a network's ability to provide performance suitable for an application's needs. Applications do not all react to delay, loss or limited bandwidth in the same way.

For example, a file transfer can often wait for retransmission, while a live conversation becomes awkward if packets arrive too late. Video streaming may need sustained throughput, and an interactive game is sensitive to delay.

2. Main QoS performance measures

📶 Bandwidth / Throughput

Bandwidth is the data-carrying capacity available on a link, usually expressed in bit/s. Throughput is the rate actually achieved by a flow or application.

Why it matters: high-volume video, downloads and backups need enough sustained capacity.

⏱️ Delay (Latency)

The time taken for data to travel from source to destination. It may include processing, queuing, transmission and propagation delay.

Why it matters: interactive voice, gaming and remote control become less responsive when delay is high.

〰️ Jitter

Variation in packet delay: packets that should arrive at regular intervals instead arrive with uneven spacing.

Why it matters: voice and real-time media can sound or appear uneven; a receiver may use a buffer to smooth timing variation.

📦 Packet loss

The fraction or number of packets that fail to reach the destination. Loss can result from congestion, transmission errors or other failures.

Why it matters: loss may trigger retransmissions in data applications and can create gaps or artefacts in real-time media.

Remember: High bandwidth does not automatically mean low delay. A link can have high capacity yet experience long queues, delay variation or packet loss during overload.

4. Understanding the trade-offs

  • Throughput versus delay: keeping a link highly utilized can improve resource use, but heavy queues may increase delay.
  • Reliability versus timeliness: retransmitting lost data helps correctness but may add delay. For real-time traffic, a late retransmission may not help the current conversation.
  • Buffering versus responsiveness: a larger playback buffer can reduce interruptions caused by jitter, but it may increase start-up or interaction delay.
  • Priority versus fairness: prioritizing delay-sensitive traffic can protect it during congestion, but lower-priority traffic still needs sensible resource allocation.

*Simplified illustration, not a strict classification. Streaming can demand high throughput while buffering makes it less sensitive to short-term delay variation than interactive communication.

6. Exam-ready answers

3 marks — Define QoS and list its parameters.

Quality of Service (QoS) is the ability of a network to provide performance suitable for an application's requirements. Important measures include bandwidth/throughput, delay (latency), jitter (variation in delay) and packet loss. Different applications assign different importance to these measures.

5 marks — Explain the QoS requirements of different applications.

Applications have different traffic characteristics. File transfer needs correct and complete delivery and can usually tolerate delay. Web and email need responsive service and reliable delivery. VoIP and video conferencing are sensitive to delay and jitter because late packets reduce interactive quality. Video streaming needs sustained throughput but can use buffering to smooth variation. Online gaming is highly sensitive to delay and may be affected by jitter and loss. Therefore, a network should consider application needs when allocating resources.

For full marks: add the four QoS measures and draw the applications → QoS mechanisms → shared network diagram.

Show quick answer hints
  • Jitter: variation in packet delay.
  • Bandwidth vs throughput: available link capacity versus achieved data rate.
  • VoIP: late packets may arrive after their useful playback time; delay and jitter disrupt conversation.
  • Streaming: a playback buffer absorbs some arrival-time variation, at the cost of extra start-up delay.
  • High throughput but poor voice: throughput alone does not show latency, jitter or loss.

8. One-minute revision

  • QoS: performance that fits application needs.
  • Bandwidth: available capacity.
  • Throughput: achieved delivery rate.
  • Delay: time to deliver data.
  • Jitter: variation in delay.
  • Packet loss: packets that fail to arrive.
  • Bulk transfer: prioritizes correctness/completeness; often tolerates delay.
  • Interactive real-time traffic: strongly affected by delay and jitter.
  • Streaming: needs sustained throughput; buffering can smooth variation.
  • Core idea: one network performance profile does not suit every application equally.

📘 QoS framework from the PPT

The lecture identifies four broad QoS design issues: (1) determine application needs; (2) regulate the traffic entering the network; (3) reserve router resources to provide the required performance; and (4) decide safely whether more traffic can be accepted.

A flow is a stream of packets from a source to a destination and may use connection-oriented or connectionless service. The four main QoS parameters are bandwidth, delay, jitter and loss.

Overprovisioning can give low loss and delay, but it is expensive and depends on predicting the traffic that will arrive. Check the PPT’s application-requirements figure/table directly for its precise example values; this note does not invent values that could not be reliably extracted.
