Traffic Throttling

Regulating the rate at which traffic enters or passes through a network to reduce overload and help keep resource use within acceptable limits.

Congestion managementRate regulationTraffic burstsExam-ready notes

1. What is traffic throttling?

Traffic throttling is the deliberate limiting or regulation of a traffic flow's sending or forwarding rate. It may be used when a source sends faster than the network or service can handle, or when traffic must comply with a configured rate or service policy.

Core idea: Control how quickly packets are injected into the network so that offered load does not overwhelm available resources.

Depending on the system, throttling may be triggered by congestion feedback or applied according to a configured policy. It can limit a sender's rate, restrict a flow at an edge device, or regulate traffic at another point in the network.

2. Visual working

  1. Traffic is generated by a source or application.
  2. A rate-control mechanism observes or enforces a traffic limit.
  3. If the flow exceeds the allowed rate, packets may be delayed, paced, marked, or otherwise managed according to the mechanism.
  4. The controlled traffic enters the network at a rate intended to reduce overload or meet policy requirements.

The exact behaviour depends on the mechanism. Throttling does not always mean dropping packets: some mechanisms delay or pace packets, while others may reject or discard excess traffic.

3. Why is throttling needed?

  • Prevent overload: reduce excessive traffic offered to a bottleneck.
  • Control bursts: prevent short bursts from overflowing queues.
  • Fair resource use: enforce per-flow or per-user limits where required.
  • Policy compliance: keep traffic within an agreed or configured rate.
  • Protect service quality: reduce the risk that one flow consumes resources needed by other flows.

4. Simple rate example

Suppose a service policy limits a flow to 4 Mb/s, but the source attempts to send at 7 Mb/s.

ItemRateInterpretation
Source's offered rate7 Mb/sTraffic the sender tries to inject
Configured limit4 Mb/sMaximum permitted rate under this example policy
Excess offered rate3 Mb/sDifference between offered rate and the configured limit

The throttling mechanism must handle the excess according to its design—for example, pace packets over time, queue them, mark them, or drop excess traffic if the policy requires it.

Excess offered rate = offered rate − permitted rate
7 Mb/s − 4 Mb/s = 3 Mb/s

This arithmetic illustrates rates only. It does not imply that every throttling mechanism handles the excess in the same way.

5. Traffic throttling and traffic shaping

These terms are related, but it is useful to distinguish their emphasis for study.

AspectTraffic throttlingTraffic shaping
EmphasisLimit or reduce the rate of a flow.Regulate the pattern of traffic over time, often by smoothing bursts to conform to a traffic profile.
Typical behaviourEnforce a rate ceiling; depending on implementation, may slow, queue, mark, or drop excess traffic.Often buffers and delays packets so output conforms to a configured rate or burst allowance.
PurposeReduce excessive load or enforce policy.Control average rate and burstiness and help meet traffic-profile requirements.

Terminology varies by textbook and implementation. A course may describe throttling as one of the mechanisms used to regulate traffic; use the exact distinction made in your PPT if it differs.

6. Advantages and limitations

Advantages

  • Can reduce overload at bottlenecks.
  • Helps enforce traffic-rate policies.
  • Can limit bursts that would otherwise build large queues.
  • May help share resources more predictably across flows.

Limitations

  • Excessive throttling can reduce application throughput or increase completion time.
  • Queued packets may experience additional delay.
  • Dropping excess packets can cause loss and possible retransmissions.
  • A fixed rate limit may not respond well to changing network conditions unless adapted appropriately.

7. Exam-ready answers

3-mark answer: Define traffic throttling.

Traffic throttling is the deliberate regulation or limitation of a traffic flow's sending or forwarding rate. It helps prevent excessive offered load, enforce rate policies, and reduce the risk of congestion at network bottlenecks.

5-mark answer: Explain the working of traffic throttling.

A traffic source generates packets and a rate-control mechanism enforces a permitted rate or reacts to congestion information. If the offered traffic exceeds the allowed rate, the mechanism controls the excess according to its design—for example, by pacing or delaying packets, queueing them, marking them, or dropping them. The controlled traffic is then forwarded at a rate intended to reduce overload or meet the configured policy.

📘 Feedback mechanisms from the lecture

What a router can monitor

  • Output-link utilization.
  • Queue length or buffer occupancy.
  • Packet loss caused by insufficient buffer space.
  • Queueing delay, emphasized in the PPT as especially useful because it can rise sharply during bursts.

Feedback should arrive in time to help, and the network needs to identify the traffic sources responsible for congestion.

Choke packets, ECN and hop-by-hop backpressure

  • Choke packet: a router selects a packet and sends a warning to its source naming the destination; the source reduces traffic to that destination by 50% in the lecture example.
  • Explicit Congestion Notification (ECN): instead of generating a separate warning packet, a router marks the packet. The receiver then informs the sender in its reply; the PPT says two IP-header bits record congestion experience.
  • Hop-by-hop backpressure: a distant signal takes time to reach the source, so packets may keep arriving. Applying the choke effect at each hop can relieve the congested point more quickly.

📌 PPT feedback mechanisms

Routers can monitor output-link utilization, queued-packet buffering, and packet loss from insufficient buffers. The PPT identifies queueing delay as especially useful because it rises sharply during bursts. A choke packet tells the source which destination is congested; the source reduces traffic to that destination by 50% in the slide example. With ECN, a router marks a forwarded packet rather than generating a separate warning; the destination informs the sender in its reply, and two IP-header bits record congestion experience. Hop-by-hop backpressure applies the choke effect at intermediate hops for faster local relief.
