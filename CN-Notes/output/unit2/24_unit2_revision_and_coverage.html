2. Congestion control: the big picture

Prevent overload

Admission control; traffic-aware routing; traffic shaping; traffic throttling.

Manage overload

Load shedding and congestion-control feedback mechanisms.

Allocate service

Packet scheduling and QoS architectures such as IntServ and DiffServ.

These mechanisms are related but not interchangeable. A useful answer states the problem each mechanism addresses and when it is applied.

3. Must-know comparison table

MechanismMain purposeQuick distinction

Admission controlDecide whether a new flow/request can be accepted.Accept/reject before committing resources.
Traffic throttlingLimit or reduce the rate of traffic entering or traversing a network.Rate restriction.
Load sheddingDiscard selected packets when resources are overloaded.Drop traffic to keep the system operating.
Traffic shapingRegulate packet transmission rate and burstiness.Delay/buffer or regulate traffic to fit a profile.

5. Numerical practice checklist

  • Traffic shaping: be able to explain token bucket and leaky bucket; identify how burst traffic is handled.
  • Admission control: calculate remaining capacity and decide whether a new request fits the stated policy.
  • Load shedding: distinguish offered arrivals, service capacity, buffered packets, and actual drops.
  • Packet fragmentation: subtract the IP header from total length, calculate fragment payloads, and express offsets in 8-byte units.
Calculation habit: write assumptions and units before calculating. Do not infer dropped packet count from arrival rate minus service capacity unless the question states the time interval and buffer behaviour.

6. Diagram practice

  1. Draw a congestion queue growing in front of a bottleneck link.
  2. Draw the traffic-shaping flow: bursty input → shaper/bucket → regulated output.
  3. Draw packet scheduling: separate queues → scheduler → output link.
  4. Draw IntServ reservation: request → admission check → accept/reject → resource handling.
  5. Draw DiffServ: ingress classification/marking → core routers apply PHB → egress.
  6. Draw tunneling: inner packet → encapsulation → tunnel network → decapsulation.
  7. Draw fragmentation: oversized datagram → smaller fragments → destination reassembly.

Use labels, arrows, and a one-line explanation under each diagram. Keep the diagram consistent with the exact model taught in class.

7. Short-answer self-test

Q1. How does admission control differ from load shedding?

Admission control decides whether to accept a new request; load shedding discards selected packets during overload.

Q2. How do shaping and scheduling differ?

Shaping regulates when/rate traffic enters the network; scheduling decides which queued packet is transmitted next.

Q3. What is the main distinction between IntServ and DiffServ?

IntServ typically manages resources per flow; DiffServ applies treatment to traffic classes/aggregates.

Q4. Does tunneling inherently encrypt packets?

No. Tunneling encapsulates packets; encryption requires a separate security mechanism.

Q5. What does the IPv4 Fragment Offset measure?

The fragment payload's position in the original payload, in units of 8 bytes.

Q6. Why can per-flow reservation be difficult at large scale?

It requires routers to maintain and manage state for many individual flows.

Q7. What does a packet scheduler determine?

Which queued packet or traffic queue gets served next.
